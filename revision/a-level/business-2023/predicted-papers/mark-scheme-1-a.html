Question 16 [4 marks]

Answer

(a) 48,000 / 40 = 1,200 units per worker per year. (b) Total labour cost = 40 x £27,600 = £1,104,000; £1,104,000 / 48,000 = £23.00 per unit.

Mark allocation
  • 1 mark: correct method for labour productivity.
  • 1 mark: 1,200 units per worker.
  • 1 mark: total labour cost £1,104,000 (or wage / productivity).
  • 1 mark: £23.00 per unit (unit required).

Own figure rule applies to follow-through errors.

Answer

Question 18 [9 marks]

Indicative content
  • Lower inflation can mean real incomes recover (wages rising faster than prices) - furniture is a discretionary, income elastic purchase, so sales volume may rise.
  • The Bank of England may cut interest rates when inflation falls - cheaper credit makes 'buy now pay later' and finance deals more attractive, boosting demand for big-ticket items.
  • Supplier and import price rises slow, making costs easier to forecast and protecting gross profit margin if retail prices are held.
  • Wage pressure eases, so employee costs as a % of turnover may stabilise.
  • BUT lower inflation reduces the ability to raise prices - if the business relied on price rises to grow revenue, revenue growth may slow.
  • Inventory bought earlier at higher prices may need discounting if rivals cut prices - margins could be squeezed in the short term.
  • Impact depends on exchange rates: imported costs depend more on the value of the pound than on UK inflation.

Section C

Question 21 [25 marks]

'Herzberg's two-factor theory shows that pay will never motivate employees.' Do you agree? Justify your view.

Arguments supporting the statement / view
  • Herzberg classed salary as a hygiene factor - poor pay causes dissatisfaction, but raising it removes dissatisfaction rather than creating lasting motivation.
  • Pay rises quickly become expected; motivators such as achievement, recognition and responsibility create intrinsic motivation that lasts.
  • Evidence of well-paid but disengaged staff (e.g. high turnover in some banks) supports the view.
Counter-arguments
  • Herzberg's research was on accountants and engineers - for low-paid workers pay meets physiological needs (Maslow) and can motivate effort.
  • Performance-related pay, commission and piece rate link pay to achievement and recognition - pay can act as recognition (a motivator).
  • Taylor's view: in repetitive work with measurable output, piece rate raises productivity.
  • 'Never' is too strong - context (job type, labour market, cost of living) matters.
Evaluation - what a top-level judgement looks like

Best answers distinguish between pay preventing dissatisfaction and pay used as recognition of achievement; judge that pay alone rarely sustains motivation but can motivate when linked to performance or when pay is low - it depends on the job and the employee.

Question 22 [25 marks]

To what extent is the Tannenbaum Schmidt continuum useful in helping managers choose the most effective leadership style?

Arguments supporting the statement / view
  • It shows leadership as a range from manager-centred (tells/sells) to subordinate-centred (consults/joins) rather than a fixed type, encouraging managers to adapt.
  • Forces in the manager, the subordinates and the situation (time, skills, nature of the problem) give a practical checklist for choosing a style.
  • Helps explain why autocratic styles suit emergencies or unskilled teams while democratic styles suit skilled, motivated staff (e.g. software developers).
Counter-arguments
  • It describes styles but does not tell managers which is 'best' - judgement is still needed.
  • Assumes managers can switch style easily; personality and organisational culture (e.g. power culture) constrain this.
  • Ignores other influences such as the leader's relationship with the team, national culture and employee motivation theories.
  • Effectiveness depends on implementation, not on knowing the model.
Evaluation - what a top-level judgement looks like

A strong conclusion weighs its value as a framework for thinking about the situation against its limits as a decision tool - most useful for managers who are aware of their own default style and the team's capability.

Question 23 [25 marks]

To what extent is Porter's five forces the most useful tool for a business deciding whether to enter a new market?

Arguments supporting the statement / view
  • Shows the likely profitability of the market - high barriers to entry, low buyer/supplier power, few substitutes and low rivalry indicate attractive returns.
  • Identifies which forces to manage (e.g. securing suppliers, differentiating to reduce buyer power).
  • Explains why some growing markets are still unprofitable (e.g. food delivery: intense rivalry, powerful customers).
Counter-arguments
  • It is a static snapshot - forces change quickly (technology lowered entry barriers for challenger banks).
  • Ignores the business's own strengths - SWOT/core competences matter: can WE compete?
  • Financial tools (investment appraisal, break-even) and market research on demand are also needed.
  • PESTLE factors (legislation, exchange rates) outside the five forces can dominate, e.g. tariffs.
Evaluation - what a top-level judgement looks like

Five forces is valuable for judging market attractiveness, but whether to enter depends on combining it with internal analysis and financial forecasts; 'most useful' depends on the market (fast-changing tech v stable industries).

Question 24 [25 marks]

Will selling through several channels (multi-channel distribution) always make a business more competitive than selling through a single channel? Justify your view.

Arguments supporting the statement / view
  • Reaches more customers and segments (online, stores, marketplaces, wholesalers) - higher sales volume and economies of scale.
  • Convenience (click and collect, returns in store) improves customer experience and loyalty.
  • Spreads risk - if one channel weakens (high street decline) others continue.
Counter-arguments
  • Higher costs: logistics, IT, staff and inventory across channels - can reduce margins and competitiveness on price.
  • Channel conflict - retailers may object if a manufacturer sells direct; inconsistent pricing damages brand.
  • Some businesses compete best by focusing on one channel (online-only lower costs; luxury brands control experience through own stores).
  • Requires integration (inventory visibility, systems) - poorly run multi-channel can be worse than a well-run single channel.
Evaluation - what a top-level judgement looks like

'Always' is unlikely: multi-channel improves competitiveness when the business can integrate channels profitably and customers value choice; for niche or low-cost positioning a single channel may be superior.
